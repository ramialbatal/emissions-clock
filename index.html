How we calculate this

Official inventories report emissions per year, not per second. When this page opens it downloads the latest annual figures for (currently up to ), projects them to using each country's latest year-on-year change ( to , capped at 20% a year), and spreads the result evenly over the year:

growth   = E[latest] / E[latest - 1] - 1
annual   = E[latest] × (1 + growth)^(this year - latest)
per sec  = annual ÷ seconds in this year
so far   = annual × fraction of the year elapsed (UTC)

Real emissions vary by season, weekday and time of day, so the live counters are a model, not a measurement. Germany is shown separately but is also part of the EU.

What the CO₂ figures cover: territorial carbon dioxide from burning fossil fuels and making cement. Land-use change (such as deforestation) is excluded, and international aviation and shipping only appear in the world total.

What the methane figures cover: methane (CH₄) from all human sources: fossil fuel production and leaks, livestock, rice farming, landfills, and land use and fires. Tonnes of methane are converted to CO₂-equivalents (CO₂e): the amount of CO₂ that would cause the same warming over 100 years (one tonne of methane ≈ 27 to 30 t CO₂e). Methane only lasts about 12 years in the air but traps far more heat while it does, so cutting it is the fastest way to slow warming in the near term. Official methane data is published with a longer delay than CO₂, so its projection reaches further ahead.

How CO₂ warms the planet

What it is

Carbon dioxide is the gas released when we burn coal, oil and gas (in power plants, cars, planes, factories and heating) and when we make cement. Plants and oceans absorb some of it, but we now release far more than they can take up.

Why it matters most

It piles up. Much of the CO₂ we emit today will still be in the air in hundreds of years, some of it for thousands. So every tonne adds to the blanket, and the blanket only gets thicker while we keep emitting. Since the 1800s, CO₂ in the air has risen from about 280 to 425 parts per million.

That’s why CO₂ has caused the largest share of global warming, and why warming only stops when CO₂ emissions reach net zero. Slowing them down isn’t enough on its own.

💡 Think of a bathtub with the tap running: turning the tap down slows the rise, but the water keeps getting higher until the tap is off.

What it is

Methane is the main ingredient of natural gas. Humans release it from leaky oil, gas and coal operations, cows and sheep (it forms in their stomachs), rice paddies, and rotting waste in landfills.

Why it matters

It’s powerful but short-lived. A tonne of methane traps about 80 times more heat than a tonne of CO₂ over its first 20 years, but it breaks down in about 12 years.

That makes it the fastest brake on warming: cut methane today and the effect shows up within a decade. It is behind roughly a third of the warming so far.

💡 If CO₂ is a blanket that keeps getting thicker, methane is a powerful heater on a timer: very strong while it’s on, but it switches itself off, as long as we stop adding more.

Why this page shows “CO₂-equivalent”

To compare gases fairly, methane is converted into the amount of CO₂ that would cause the same warming over 100 years (1 tonne of methane ≈ 28 tonnes CO₂-eq). Over 20 years the factor is about 80, so the 100-year number understates its short-term punch.
